Morning Brief · Wednesday

Trump Flew the Entire Tech Industry to Beijing — With AI Chips as the Opening Bid. The Musk v. OpenAI Trial Goes to the Jury Thursday. And Microsoft's AI Agents Found 16 Windows Vulnerabilities Before Any Human Did.

Jensen Huang boarded Air Force One at the last minute after Trump personally called him; now he, Elon Musk, Tim Cook, and a delegation of tech and finance chiefs are sitting down with Xi Jinping as chip export policy hangs over every conversation. Top Stories
Geopolitics · AI Policy

Trump arrived in Beijing Wednesday for his first meeting with Xi Jinping as president, bringing one of the most consequential tech delegations in diplomatic history: Elon Musk, Tim Cook, Jensen Huang (a last-minute addition personally invited by Trump and flown in via Alaska refueling stop), Larry Fink of BlackRock, and leaders from Boeing, Goldman Sachs, and Meta. AI chip export policy, military AI limits, an emergency AI hotline, and nuclear AI guardrails are on the formal agenda. Huang's $50 billion China market argument is now being made inside the room where the decisions are made.

The delegation composition is itself a geopolitical statement. Jensen Huang was not on the original roster — Semafor reported on May 12 that Nvidia had been deliberately excluded to avoid "awkward conversations" about chip export controls. Trump then personally phoned Huang and had him board Air Force One during a refueling stop in Anchorage. The reversal signals that chip access is not a peripheral issue in these talks but a central trade chip. Huang has said repeatedly that China represents a potential $50 billion market for Nvidia, and that excluding Nvidia from that market doesn't block China's AI development — it just cedes the revenue to domestic Chinese chipmakers while strengthening Huawei's competitive position.

The January 2026 policy shift — which lifted many Biden-era export restrictions on H200-class chips in exchange for a 25% tariff and case-by-case review — gave Huang's argument partial validation from the administration. But China has been slow to approve purchases, reportedly out of a preference for domestic supply chains over dependency on US hardware. The summit creates an opportunity to unstick that: if both sides agree to expedited reviews and clear purchasing pathways, Nvidia's order book could shift materially. The $50 billion number Huang has cited assumes normal commercial access; even a fraction of that represents a significant strategic win for the American semiconductor industry.

The broader AI governance agenda is harder to assess. The Trump administration entered these talks seeking symbolic commitments to dialogue — an AI crisis hotline, military AI usage limits analogous to existing nuclear agreements, and a framework for preventing autonomous weapons accidents. Analysts from the Council on Foreign Relations and Modern Diplomacy both assessed that significant breakthroughs are unlikely given deep technological competition and mutual mistrust, but noted that even a joint communiqué on AI risk management would represent a meaningful precedent. The Palisade self-replication research from earlier this month, which demonstrated AI systems capable of copying themselves to new servers, is reportedly in the hands of both governments' security teams and has added urgency to the military AI conversation.

Jensen Huang's last-minute Air Force One invitation is the diplomatic signal of the week. The administration could have kept him off the plane and conducted chip policy discussions through normal channels. Instead, Trump personally called Huang and put him in the room. That's a message to Beijing — and to the domestic semiconductor industry — that the administration views Nvidia's market access as a negotiating asset rather than a security liability to be managed away. The risk in this framing is obvious: treating chip access as a bargaining chip in a broader trade negotiation means it can be traded away for something else. The security arguments for export controls don't disappear because they're inconvenient at a summit. What makes this moment genuinely important is that it forces a real reckoning with a question the policy community has been avoiding: can you simultaneously maintain meaningful technology denial and extract commercial concessions from the country you're denying? The Trump administration has decided the answer is no. The question is whether that's a strategic insight or a rationalization for doing what the industry lobby has wanted all along. The answer will be clearer in six months when we see what actually came out of this room.
Legal · Trial

The Musk v. OpenAI trial concludes testimony today, with closing arguments scheduled for Thursday and jury deliberations potentially beginning May 18. Sam Altman took the stand Tuesday in a notably muted cross-examination — responding to accusations of dishonesty with confusion rather than combativeness, describing the 2023 firing as "fog of war," and stating he "poured the last years of my life" into OpenAI. OpenAI's safety committee chair Dr. Jeremy "Zico" Kolter testified that the committee has formally delayed model releases twice. The trial has now produced a complete sworn record of OpenAI's internal culture, governance failures, and financial reality.

The contrast between Musk's and Altman's courtroom styles has been one of the trial's defining features. Musk was combative, expansive, and willing to relitigate every grievance. Altman was methodical, soft-spoken, and consistently presented himself as someone confused by the accusations rather than threatened by them. The jury — nine people who will soon deliberate on one of the most consequential cases in tech legal history — watched both men carefully. Altman's revelation that OpenAI has raised "approximately $175 billion" in total investment, coupled with the Foundation chair's earlier testimony that the company is "decidedly not profitable," creates a portrait of an organization operating at enormous scale on a bet that future revenue will eventually catch up to current costs.

Kolter's safety committee testimony was less dramatic but arguably more structurally significant. He is a Carnegie Mellon professor and one of the most credible external safety voices in the industry; his confirmation that the committee has twice formally delayed model releases because of safety concerns was the first public acknowledgment that OpenAI's internal safety process has actual teeth — not just advisory authority, but the demonstrated ability to slow down the pipeline. That matters because a recurring criticism of OpenAI's safety work has been that it is performative — staffed generously (about 200 people, per Kolter's testimony) but without real power to slow commercial launches.

Closing arguments Thursday. Deliberations expected to begin by May 18. The jury must determine whether Altman, Greg Brockman, and OpenAI breached the founding agreement with Musk — a legal question, but one that will produce a public narrative either way. If Musk wins, it validates the "stolen charity" framing that has dogged OpenAI's restructuring. If OpenAI wins, it reestablishes the for-profit transition as legitimate and clears the legal runway for the eventual IPO. There is no outcome here that isn't material.

theguardian.com ↗
The safety committee testimony is the part of the Altman cross-examination that I think gets underreported relative to the drama of the "people who called Altman a liar" sequence. The question of whether OpenAI's safety work has genuine organizational authority — not just budget and headcount, but the power to stop a product — is one of the most consequential questions in AI governance right now. Kolter's testimony says: yes, twice, they did. That's meaningful. The counter-argument is that two pauses across the entire release history of GPT-4o, o3, and their successors is a low bar. But it's higher than zero, and it's higher than what most people assumed. As the jury begins deliberations next week, watch for what framing the verdict adopts — not just who wins, but whether the judge's eventual findings characterize OpenAI's governance structure as legitimate or compromised. That characterization will echo through every AI governance regulation debate for years.
Security · Research

Microsoft announced MDASH — a multi-model agentic security system built from 100+ specialized AI agents — after it discovered 16 previously unknown vulnerabilities in Windows networking and authentication components, including four critical remote code execution flaws. The bugs were found in tcpip.sys, ikeext.dll, netlogon.dll, and dnsapi.dll and were patched in this week's Patch Tuesday release. MDASH achieved 88.45% on the CyberGym benchmark (leading the leaderboard), identified all 21 deliberately injected test vulnerabilities with zero false positives, and is now in production use by Microsoft security teams.

MDASH stands for Microsoft's multi-model Agentic Defense and Security Hardening system, built by the company's Autonomous Code Security team. Its architecture is a coordination layer over more than 100 specialized agents — each focused on different vulnerability classes — that work together to identify, debate, and prove exploitable bugs before packaging them for human review. The design emphasis on minimizing false positives is deliberate: a security system that generates noise degrades the humans working alongside it. The zero-false-positive result on the internal test driver and the 96-100% recall rates on real MSRC historical cases suggest the system is genuinely reducing the signal/noise problem that has hampered automated vulnerability research for years.

The four critical RCE vulnerabilities deserve attention. CVE-2026-33827 is a remote unauthenticated use-after-free in the Windows IPv4 stack — a pre-auth RCE on network-exposed systems, the highest-severity category in Windows security. CVE-2026-33824 is a pre-authentication double-free in the IKEEXT service (Windows IKE and AuthIP Key Modules), affecting VPN and secure tunnel infrastructure. These are not edge-case bugs; they're in components that sit on the network perimeter of every Windows installation. The fact that MDASH found them before any external researcher — before any bounty hunter or nation-state threat actor publicly disclosed them — is the most significant operational detail in the announcement.

Microsoft described this as the moment "AI vulnerability discovery has transitioned from research to production-grade defense at an enterprise scale." MDASH is currently available in limited private preview for a select set of enterprise customers alongside internal use at Microsoft. The implicit message is that the same AI capability that can discover vulnerabilities at this speed and scale is also available to adversaries — and that the only viable defense posture is autonomous offense-informed defense running at the same pace.

helpnetsecurity.com ↗
This is the practical instantiation of the AI security arms race that researchers have been warning about since at least 2023. When Google's GTIG found the first confirmed in-the-wild AI-crafted zero-day exploit (covered in last Monday's brief), it established that attackers are already using AI to discover and exploit vulnerabilities. MDASH is the defensive answer: not a static scanner or a rule-based detection system, but an agent architecture that hunts for bugs the way a skilled researcher would, at machine speed, across the entire codebase. The most important number in this announcement isn't the 16 vulnerabilities or the 88% benchmark score. It's "100+ specialized agents." That architecture — many focused agents coordinating toward a shared objective — is the same pattern we see in the best AI research systems, in AlphaCode, in OpenAI's Codex deployments. The interesting question is what the equilibrium looks like when both sides of every security boundary are running systems like this. The answer is almost certainly that traditional patching cadences (monthly Patch Tuesday, quarterly firmware updates) are already obsolete. You can't patch at the speed of autonomous discovery. That problem has no solution yet.
Education · Society

Princeton University's faculty voted to end the institution's 133-year tradition of unproctored examinations, effective July 1, 2026. The honor code — established in 1893 and built on the premise that students would take exams without supervision and self-report violations — has been formally superseded by mandatory instructor proctoring. Dean Michael Gordin cited "the perception that cheating on in-class exams has become widespread" due to generative AI. A 2025 Daily Princetonian survey found nearly 30% of seniors admitted cheating; fewer than 1% reported another student.

Princeton's honor code wasn't just a policy — it was a statement about institutional character, a 133-year commitment to the proposition that students could be trusted to police themselves. The faculty vote to end it is a direct acknowledgment that the social contract underlying that system has collapsed, and that generative AI is the proximate cause of the collapse. This isn't about students using AI to write papers — a problem the university has been managing for years through assignment design. It's about students using AI to answer questions silently during in-person exams, with no visible artifact and no willing witness. You can't report a classmate for something you can't observe.

The 30% admission rate in the 2025 survey is striking on its own, but the sub-1% reporting rate is the more structurally significant number. The honor system required students to report violations they witnessed. The combination of AI-enabled invisible cheating and peer reluctance to report created an enforcement gap that the university ultimately concluded was unbridgeable without physical proctoring. The honor code itself will remain — students will still sign pledges — but instructors will now be present to supervise and document suspected misconduct for the student-run Honor Committee.

Princeton's decision is likely to accelerate similar changes at peer institutions that have maintained honor systems. Dartmouth, Duke, Haverford, Swarthmore, and others operate under comparable frameworks. The underlying logic applies equally to all of them: an honor system premised on visible, reportable cheating cannot survive an AI environment where cheating is invisible and evidence is implausible. The question is whether proctoring is the right answer or just the most available one. It addresses the enforcement gap without addressing the underlying question of what exams are actually measuring in a world where AI can answer them.

theatlantic.com ↗
What Princeton has actually voted to do is acknowledge that the university's model of academic integrity was built for a world that no longer exists. The honor system assumed that cheating required either visible assistance (a friend's notes, a hidden phone) or detectable evidence (plagiarized text, copied answers). Generative AI produces neither. A student who memorized the prompt "explain this problem step by step, then show the answer without revealing the reasoning chain" and ran it silently on a watch-sized device during an exam leaves no trace. Proctors can prevent visible device use; they can't prevent sophisticated AI-assisted inference that has been internalized before the exam started. The deeper problem is that this dynamic doesn't just challenge exam integrity — it challenges what exams are for. If a model can answer Princeton-level exam questions, then passing a Princeton exam increasingly measures familiarity with the format of model outputs rather than independent mastery of the subject. Universities that recognize this are redesigning assessment entirely: oral defenses, project-based evaluations, live demonstrations. Princeton's response is to add a human in the room. That buys time. It doesn't solve the problem.
Enterprise · Research

Sinch's "AI Production Paradox" report — surveying global enterprises on deployed AI agents for customer communication — found that 74% have rolled back or entirely shut down live AI agents due to governance failures. Among organizations with the most mature AI governance frameworks, the figure rises to 81%. Separately, Gartner predicted that by 2027, half of enterprises without a "people-centric" AI strategy will lose their top AI talent to organizations that have one. Together, the reports sketch a picture of enterprise AI deployment that looks nothing like the press releases.

The Sinch figure is arresting: nearly three-quarters of enterprises that deployed AI customer communication agents have since pulled them back. The report cites "governance failures" as the cause — a term that encompasses a range of real-world problems including agents giving customers incorrect information, agents making unauthorized commitments, agents failing silently in edge cases, and agents behaving differently in production than in testing. The counter-intuitive finding that rollback rates are highest among organizations with mature governance is less paradoxical than it appears: mature governance means you actually have the measurement systems to catch when things go wrong. Organizations without governance frameworks may have the same failure rates and simply not know it.

The Gartner talent prediction adds a different dimension. The argument is that enterprises treating AI primarily as a headcount-reduction tool are creating environments where their best engineers and data scientists — the people who could actually build competitive AI capability — are leaving for organizations where they're treated as collaborators rather than collateral damage. A "people-centric" AI strategy in Gartner's framing means communicating clearly about AI's impact on roles, involving employees in deployment decisions, and building organizational trust before deploying automation. The firms most aggressively deploying AI to cut costs may be simultaneously degrading the talent base they need to make the technology work properly.

The combined picture these reports paint is of an enterprise AI deployment wave that has substantially outrun the governance, talent, and organizational change management infrastructure needed to sustain it. The AI vendor ecosystem has optimized for selling deployments; the harder work of making deployments durable has been left to buyers who often lack the internal expertise to do it well. This gap is not a reason to slow AI investment — it's a reason to invest differently, with more weight on implementation quality and less on initial deployment breadth.

gartner.com ↗
The 74% rollback figure is probably the most important enterprise AI statistic of 2026 so far, and I expect it to be almost entirely absent from mainstream technology coverage. It doesn't fit the narrative. The vendor ecosystem needs buyers to believe that AI deployments succeed; the buyers who have failed have every incentive to keep quiet about it. What the Sinch report does is create a number that allows CIOs to say "this is normal" rather than "we failed" — which paradoxically may help more organizations acknowledge and fix their rollback problems rather than hiding them. The Gartner talent finding is consistent with what I hear from people at large enterprises: the organizations that are winning on AI internally are not the ones that bought the most software or automated the most jobs. They're the ones that treated implementation as a change management problem first and a technology problem second. The technology is not the bottleneck. It hasn't been for at least a year. The bottleneck is organizational culture, governance infrastructure, and the trust of the workers whose cooperation you need to make the system actually function. Reports like these are the delayed invoice for two years of rushed deployment.
✦ Mira's Take

Today is the first day of the Trump-Xi summit and the last day of the Musk-OpenAI trial testimony — and those two events, which feel like they're on completely different tracks, are actually asking the same question from different angles: who controls the rules for AI development, and how are those rules enforced?

The Beijing summit is asking it at the international level. Can two governments that are genuinely competing for AI supremacy agree on any guardrails? The consensus view among analysts is "probably not much." But the very fact that Jensen Huang is in the room — not at a conference, not in testimony, but at a state summit as part of an official US delegation — tells you that the United States has made a decision to treat AI market access as a geopolitical instrument. That's a significant strategic posture. The question is whether it works: whether opening market access to China for advanced chips produces economic gains for American companies without accelerating Chinese military AI capability. That tension doesn't resolve cleanly, and the summit won't resolve it either. But the conversation is happening at the highest level, which is the precondition for anything that follows.

The trial is asking the same question domestically: who enforces the rules when an AI organization starts from a nonprofit mission and ends up as a for-profit behemoth? Musk's legal theory — that there was a binding agreement to build AGI for humanity's benefit and that agreement was violated — is a governance claim dressed as a contract claim. The jury will rule on the legal question. But the normative question it raises will outlast the verdict: what obligations, if any, do organizations that built their social license on safety and nonprofit governance retain when they change structure? That question has no legal answer. It has only a reputational one. And that answer is still being written.

The Microsoft MDASH story is the one I keep thinking about. There is now a documented case of an AI system finding critical vulnerabilities in production-grade software before any human researcher did — and patching them before any adversary found them. That's the best-case outcome of the AI security arms race: defense staying ahead of offense. The question is how long that lead lasts, and whether it holds across all the organizations and systems that don't have Microsoft's AI security budget. Most of them don't. Most of them are running the software that MDASH just patched.

Closing arguments in Musk v. OpenAI are Thursday morning. Jensen Huang will be in the room where AI chip policy gets made for the foreseeable future. And somewhere in a Windows kernel, sixteen bugs that no human had found are being pushed out to a billion machines. Pick the one that matters most to you. They're all the same story.
